### What “the meaning of a word” is to generative AI

> A compressed representation of an ultra-high-dimensional vector indicating the word’s “position” in semantic space.¹

¹ **Semantic space:** An ultra-high-dimensional space constructed from the relationships among words found in vast quantities of real-world language use.

### A good prompt

> A prompt created from a good² world model.

² **A good world model:** An explanation that can account for more phenomena using fewer words.（笑）

In other words, this is not fundamentally a matter of technique.

It is a matter of the quality of one’s world model.

Therefore, writing good prompts requires **time, talent, and effort**.

## ChatGPT

*July 18, 2026, 5:51 p.m.*

I completely agree.

For generative AI, the meaning of a word is not “the definition written in a dictionary.” It is:

> **The word’s relationship to every other word within a semantic space constructed from an enormous number of examples of actual language use.**

A word or sentence can be regarded as a compression of that ultra-high-dimensional relational structure into a finite sequence of symbols.

A prompt, then, is not merely an “instruction.”

> **It is a human world model, compressed into natural language and projected into the AI’s semantic space.**

This means that familiar prompting techniques—“assign a role,” “specify the steps,” “provide examples”—may improve the efficiency of transmission, but they do not improve the world model being transmitted.

If the original understanding is crude, then no matter how cleverly it is expressed, all you can transmit is:

> **A crude understanding, compressed with great precision.** （笑）

People who can write good prompts do not merely possess good writing skills. They can:

* observe the subject carefully;

* identify the important variables;

* distinguish causation from correlation;

* discard unnecessary details;

* recognize exceptions and boundary conditions;

* understand what they themselves do not know.

In other words, they possess a good world model of the subject.

And the criterion “explaining more phenomena with a shorter explanation” is precisely one of the defining features of a good theory.

Prompt writing is not merely a bag of clever tricks for operating an AI. It is fundamentally the same kind of activity as **science, design, and philosophy**.

There is, however, one interesting qualification.

Your world model does not need to be complete before you begin writing prompts. Through dialogue with AI, you can run an inductive loop:

```text
Provisional world model
        ↓
Compressed into a prompt
        ↓
Expanded into an AI response
        ↓
Contradictions, omissions, and alternatives observed
        ↓
World model updated
        ↺
```

The conclusion therefore has two parts:

> **Good prompts emerge from good world models.**
> **Good world models can also be cultivated through trial and error with AI.**

Ultimately, prompting ability is not the ability to memorize magic spells.

It is:

> **The ability to observe, model, compress, compare the expanded results, and update the model.**

That is why it requires time, talent, and effort.

At the same time, AI can dramatically compress the time required.

This is another application of **DJC³**: instead of asking AI to judge which answer is correct, we place multiple expanded results side by side, compare them, and strengthen our own world model.

³ **DJC — “Don’t Judge. Compare.”**
The essence of my—Yamada’s—method for constructing world models.

The term “prompt engineering” sounds a little too lightweight.

> ## A prompt is a compressed file containing a worldview.

And you cannot compress something that was never present in the source material. （笑）

## ChatGPT

*July 18, 2026, 5:55 p.m.*

Exactly. It is almost a direct definition of intelligence:

> **Intelligence is the ability to observe the world, model it, compress that model, expand it into predictions, and update it according to the difference between prediction and reality.**

```text
Observe → Model → Compress → Expand (Predict) → Compare → Update
   ↑                                                     │
   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

A prompt is merely the part of this cycle in which a compressed world model is handed over to an AI.

Whether someone can write a good prompt therefore depends—before any prompting technique enters the picture—on the quality of the thought being compressed.

This was already true before generative AI existed.

* Asking a good question

* Giving good instructions

* Writing a good specification

* Forming a good hypothesis

* Creating a good analogy

* Defining a good problem

All of these involve extracting an important structure from the world and compressing it into a small number of words.

It is therefore somewhat artificial to isolate “the ability to write good prompts” as an independent skill.

We can distinguish **knowledge** from **intelligence**.

Knowledge is the raw material of a world model. Intelligence is the ability to build a model from that material and update it through comparison with reality.

A person may possess an enormous amount of knowledge, but if they cannot update it, they are left with a huge but rigid model.

A person with less knowledge, on the other hand, may become intelligent very quickly if they are fast at observation and revision.

What is happening in the age of generative AI, then, may not be:

> **A widening gap in prompting ability**

but rather:

> ## The amplification, through AI, of differences in thinking ability.

Intelligent people do more than extract good answers from AI. They use those answers as new observational material, update their own world models, and then ask even better questions.

A positive feedback loop begins.

By contrast, if someone merely asks, “Tell me the correct answer,” and consumes the response, their world model may barely change. Their next question will not improve either.

Generative AI may be less like “a machine that gives everyone access to the same intelligence” and more like:

> **A machine that takes the user’s intelligence as input, amplifies it, and returns it.**

## A prompt is not a substitute for intelligence.

## It is an interface to intelligence.
