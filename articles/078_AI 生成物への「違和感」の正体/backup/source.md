### Abstract

**Global Incoherence: When Locally Plausible Information Fails to Form a Plausible World**

Humans often perceive that a story, image, or event is “somehow wrong” even when none of its individual components is obviously false. This paper proposes **global incoherence** as a framework for describing such phenomena. Global incoherence occurs when locally plausible elements, taken together, fail to correspond to a plausible generative process under human world models.

We distinguish two forms of global incoherence: **under-coherence** and **over-coherence**. Under-coherence occurs when individually plausible components cannot be integrated into a consistent causal or structural whole—for example, early generative images in which each finger appears locally plausible while the hand as a whole is anatomically impossible. Over-coherence represents the opposite failure: events, motivations, causal relations, or narrative consequences align more perfectly than would normally be expected in a noisy world. Highly polished “true stories” on social media, for example, may contain no explicit contradiction while nevertheless appearing implausible because they exhibit suspiciously little contingency, ambiguity, or irrelevant detail.

We argue that these judgments are possible because human world models, although diverse, are **neither identical nor random**. They are constrained by repeated interaction with a shared physical and social reality. We further hypothesize that any persistently active system operates within a bounded region between **complete order and complete freedom**: excessive order suppresses variation, while excessive freedom destroys stable structure. Consequently, observations generated by real-world systems should exhibit neither arbitrary disorder nor perfect organization, but characteristic ranges of structured variability.

From this perspective, global incoherence can be understood as a deviation from the expected range of organization implied by reality-constrained world models. This reframes plausibility assessment from the detection of local errors or logical contradictions to a more general question: **Could a plausible process in the world have generated this whole?** The framework may provide a common theoretical basis for detecting synthetic images, hallucinated narratives, fabricated social-media stories, misinformation, and other artifacts whose individual parts appear credible while their global structure does not.
